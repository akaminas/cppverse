## What it is

A fixed step is a compromise. In an epidemic, the state changes quickly while the outbreak grows and peaks, and barely at all for the months afterwards. A step small enough for the peak wastes effort on the tail; a step suited to the tail ruins the peak. An *adaptive* integrator chooses the step as it goes: small where the solution changes rapidly, large where it is smooth, so that the error of every step stays below a tolerance you set.

It needs three ingredients: an estimate of the error of each step, a rule for accepting or rejecting the step, and a rule for choosing the next step size. This lesson builds all three on top of <Concept id="runge-kutta" /> and <Concept id="numerical-error" />.

### Estimating the error without the true solution

The true solution is unknown, so the error of a step cannot be measured directly. But it can be *estimated* by taking the same step with two methods of different order. Suppose a method of order $p$ and one of order $p + 1$ both start from $\mathbf{x}$ at time $t$. Their local errors are

$$
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{lo}} = \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{lo}} - \mathbf{x}(t + h) = O(h^{p+1}),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{hi}} = \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{hi}} - \mathbf{x}(t + h) = O(h^{p+2}),
$$

where $\mathbf{x}(t + h)$ is the exact solution through $\mathbf{x}$. Subtracting,

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{lo}} - \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{hi}} = \boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{lo}} - \boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{hi}} \approx \boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\mathrm{lo}},
$$

because for small $h$ the higher-order error is negligible beside the lower-order one. The difference between the two answers, which you can compute, estimates the error of the lower-order answer, which you cannot.

Computing two methods per step would double the cost, unless they share their slope evaluations. A pair of methods built to share them is an **embedded pair**. The simplest is Euler's method inside Heun's: both start with the slope $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x})$, and Heun's method needs one more, $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{f}(t + h, \mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{a})$. Then

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Euler}} = \mathbf{x} + h\,\mathbf{a}, \qquad
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Heun}} = \mathbf{x} + \tfrac{h}{2}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b}), \qquad
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Euler}} - \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{Heun}} = \tfrac{h}{2}(\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}).
$$

The error estimate for component $i$ is $e_i = \tfrac{h}{2}\lvert b_i - a_i \rvert$, at no cost beyond the two evaluations Heun's method needs anyway. Such pairs are labelled by their orders, here Heun-Euler 2(1).

### Tolerances and the scaled error

A tolerance must mean something for every variable, large or small. Professional solvers combine an *absolute* tolerance $a_{\mathrm{tol}}$ with a *relative* one $r_{\mathrm{tol}}$ into a scale per component,

$$
s_i = a_{\mathrm{tol}} + r_{\mathrm{tol}} \max\bigl(\lvert x_i \rvert, \lvert x_{\mathrm{Heun},i} \rvert\bigr),
$$

and measure the step by its largest scaled error,

$$
E = \max_i \frac{e_i}{s_i}.
$$

$E$ is dimensionless; the step is acceptable if $E \le 1$. The relative part makes the control independent of the units: an error of $10^{-6}$ is small for a variable near 1 and large for one near $10^{-5}$. The absolute part stops the solver from demanding impossible relative accuracy from a variable that decays towards zero, such as the proportion infectious late in an epidemic. Taking the maximum over components lets the hardest variable set the step.

### Accept or reject

If $E > 1$, the step is **rejected**: time does not advance, the state is unchanged and the step is retried with a smaller $h$. If $E \le 1$, the step is **accepted** and the solution moves on. Rejected attempts are not free; they cost evaluations and are counted in the total work.

### Choosing the next step

The Euler error estimate scales as $h^2$. If a step of size $h$ produced the scaled error $E$, a step $h_{\mathrm{new}}$ would produce about $E\,(h_{\mathrm{new}}/h)^2$. Setting that equal to 1 gives the step that would just meet the tolerance, $h_{\mathrm{new}} = h/\sqrt{E}$. In general, for a lower-order method of order $p$ the exponent is $1/(p + 1)$. Used naively, that rule misbehaves, so three safeguards are standard:

- a **safety factor** (typically 0.8 to 0.9) aims a little below the predicted step, because the estimate is itself approximate and a step just at the limit is often rejected;
- **growth and shrink limits** (for example at most five times larger and at least ten times smaller per attempt) stop a tiny error estimate from producing an enormous next step, and a huge one from collapsing the step;
- a **minimum step**: if the step must shrink below a set minimum (which should lie well above the rounding error of $t$ itself), something is wrong (a singularity in the model, stiffness, or a bug), and the integrator should stop with an error rather than crawl forever.

Altogether,

$$
h_{\mathrm{new}} = h \cdot \min\Bigl(f_{\max},\ \max\bigl(f_{\min},\ \sigma / \sqrt{E}\bigr)\Bigr),
$$

with safety factor $\sigma = 0.9$, growth limit $f_{\max} = 5$ and shrink limit $f_{\min} = 0.1$. On acceptance only the growth limit matters in practice, on rejection only the shrink limit.

### Which answer to keep

The error estimate refers to the lower-order (Euler) answer, but an accepted step keeps the higher-order (Heun) answer. This practice, called *local extrapolation*, is nearly universal: the Heun value is more accurate, so the step that is actually taken is usually better than the tolerance requires. The price is that the error of the value you keep is not itself estimated. In practice that is harmless, but it is one reason the tolerance is a target, not a guarantee.

## Why it matters

Adaptive control changes what you specify. Instead of a step size, whose consequences for accuracy you can only discover by experiment, you state an accuracy and let the integrator find the steps. For models with fast and slow phases (outbreaks, pulses of drug absorption, the spike of a neuron, a bloom and crash of plankton) the savings are large, because the steps can differ by orders of magnitude between phases. And the controller doubles as a diagnostic: steps that shrink drastically point to where the model is hard to integrate.

Two caveats apply. The tolerance controls the *local* error of each step, not the *global* error at the end; the two are related, but only a run with a tighter tolerance tells you how far the result has converged. And an explicit adaptive method does not cure stiffness: on a stiff problem it will find the stability limit by trial and error and stay there (see <Concept id="numerical-stability" />).

The driver loops until the end time, clipping the last step so that the run ends exactly at 400 days.

The first trial step of one day is far too long; the controller rejects it, shrinks the step to about 0.13 days and then lets it evolve. Over the whole run it accepts 1,779 steps and rejects only 3. Steps range from 0.018 days during the outbreak to 16 days in the tail, and the step counts per 50-day window tell the same story: 1,573 steps in the first 50 days, 179 in the next 50, and between 3 and 8 in each window after that. The final proportion susceptible, 0.10720885, differs from the final-size relation's 0.10720857 by about $3 \times 10^{-7}$, below the requested tolerance of $10^{-6}$ per step.

The minimum step is enforced with an exception (see <Concept id="exceptions" />): if a rejection pushes `h` below `hMin`, `tryStep` throws, and `main` reports where the integration failed instead of looping indefinitely.

## In biological modelling

### Standard embedded pairs

The Heun-Euler pair is ideal for learning but too low in order for serious work. Two higher-order pairs dominate practice, and you will meet them under these names in every library:

- **Bogacki-Shampine 3(2)**: a third-order method with an embedded second-order estimate. It has four stages, but the last slope of an accepted step is the slope at the new point, which is exactly the first slope of the next step. Reusing it ("first same as last", FSAL) brings the cost to three evaluations per step. It suits moderate tolerances. MATLAB's `ode23` and SciPy's `RK23` are of this kind.
- **Dormand-Prince 5(4)**: a fifth-order method with a fourth-order estimate and FSAL, about six evaluations per step. It is the default general-purpose choice: MATLAB's `ode45`, SciPy's `RK45` and Boost.Odeint's `runge_kutta_dopri5` use it.

Both follow exactly the pattern of this lesson: shared stages, an error estimate from the difference, a scaled error, accept or reject, and a step update with exponent $1/(p+1)$ for the lower order $p$ (a cube root for Bogacki-Shampine, a fifth root for Dormand-Prince). Their coefficient tables are published in the original papers listed above and in standard texts.

### Use a library for production work

For research code, prefer a tested library over your own integrator:

- **Boost.Odeint** (header-only C++): fixed-step and controlled steppers, Dormand-Prince with dense output, and Rosenbrock methods for stiff systems. It works with `std::vector`, `std::array` and your own state types.
- **GNU Scientific Library (GSL)**, `gsl_odeiv2`: embedded Runge-Kutta pairs and implicit methods behind a C interface.
- **SUNDIALS** (CVODE, ARKODE, IDA): the reference for large and stiff systems, with variable-order implicit methods and sensitivity analysis.

Having written a controller yourself, you know what their options mean: absolute and relative tolerances, an initial step, a minimum step, a maximum number of steps, and what an error such as "step size too small" is telling you.

### Practical advice

- **Choose tolerances with the biology in mind.** For proportions, $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-8}$ is usually ample. For population sizes in individuals an absolute tolerance far below one individual is rarely meaningful, but a relative tolerance still is.
- **Check convergence by tightening the tolerance.** Rerun with a tolerance ten or a hundred times smaller; the quantities you report should not move appreciably.
- **Stop at discontinuities.** A dose, a harvest or a switch of season is better handled by integrating exactly to the event and restarting, than by letting the controller discover the jump through a cascade of rejections.
- **Get output at regular times** either by clipping steps so that they end at output times (simple, slightly more steps) or by interpolating between accepted steps ("dense output", provided by good libraries).

## Common mistakes

- **Advancing time on a rejected step.** A rejected attempt must leave `t` and `x` unchanged. Only `h` changes.
- **Keeping the wrong solution.** After computing the higher-order answer, propagate it; keeping the Euler value throws away accuracy you already paid for.
- **No limits on the step change.** Without a growth limit, a step with an error estimate of almost zero (for example at an equilibrium, or when two slopes happen to coincide) produces a gigantic next step. Without a safety factor many steps are rejected.
- **Purely relative tolerance.** A variable decaying towards zero then demands ever more relative accuracy and the steps never grow again.
- **No escape route.** Without a minimum step and a limit on the number of attempts, a model with a singularity or a bug can keep the program running forever. Check also that the state stays finite: once it overflows to infinity the error estimate becomes NaN, and comparisons with NaN are always false, so a careless controller accepts the step.
- **Equating the tolerance with the final error.** The tolerance controls each step; the accumulated error can be larger or smaller. Measure it.
