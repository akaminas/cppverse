## What it is

An ODE model $d\mathbf{x}/dt = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x})$ tells you the slope of the trajectory at every point (see <Concept id="odes" />). Euler's method, the simplest way to turn slopes into a trajectory, follows the current slope in a straight line for a short time, recomputes the slope, and repeats.

The method falls straight out of the definition of the derivative,

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x(t + h) - x(t)}{h}.
$$

Drop the limit and keep a small but finite step $h$. The difference quotient is then only approximately equal to the derivative, which the model gives as $f(t, x(t))$:

$$
\frac{x(t + h) - x(t)}{h} \approx f\bigl(t, x(t)\bigr)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
x(t + h) \approx x(t) + h\, f\bigl(t, x(t)\bigr).
$$

Starting from the initial value $x(t_0) = x_0$ and applying the update again and again gives approximations $x_1, x_2, \dots$ at the times $t_n = t_0 + nh$:

$$
x_{n+1} = x_n + h\, f(t_n, x_n).
$$

The symbols: $x_n$ is the approximation of the state at time $t_n$ (in the units of the state), $h$ is the step size (in units of time, for example days), and $f(t_n, x_n)$ is the rate of change at the start of the step (state units per unit time). Geometrically, each step moves along the tangent to the solution curve through the current point.

### One step by hand

Take logistic growth with $r = 0.8$ per day, $K = 100$ individuals and $N_0 = 2$. The slope at the start is $0.8 \times 2 \times (1 - 2/100) = 1.568$ individuals per day, so one Euler step of $h = 1$ day gives

$$
N_1 = 2 + 1 \times 1.568 = 3.568.
$$

The exact solution at $t = 1$ is $N(1) \approx 4.345$. Euler's estimate is too low because the true slope increases during the day as the population grows, while Euler keeps using the slope from the start of the day. That is the whole story of the method in miniature: its error comes from the slope changing within a step, and it shrinks as the step shrinks.

## Why it matters

Euler's method is rarely the best choice for serious work, but it is the template for every method that follows. The structure you build here, a model function, a stepper and a driver that advances time and writes output, stays the same when you replace the stepper by a Runge–Kutta method or an adaptive one. It is also the easiest method to reason about, so it is where the main pitfalls of numerical integration are easiest to see: lagging behind the true solution, the cost of small steps, and instability when steps are too large (see <Concept id="numerical-stability" />).

The update $x_{n+1} = x_n + h f(t_n, x_n)$ also looks exactly like a discrete-time model (see <Concept id="time-stepping" />). That resemblance is a warning as much as a convenience. A discrete-time model *is* the biology: its time step is a generation or a breeding season. In Euler's method the step is an artefact of the approximation, and nothing in the results should depend on it once it is small enough.

### All variables move together

For a system, the update is a vector equation: every component of $\mathbf{x}_{n+1}$ uses the *old* state $\mathbf{x}_n$.

$$
S_{n+1} = S_n - h\,\beta S_n I_n, \qquad
I_{n+1} = I_n + h\,(\beta S_n I_n - \gamma I_n), \qquad
R_{n+1} = R_n + h\,\gamma I_n.
$$

The safe way to guarantee this is the one used above: compute the whole vector of derivatives first, then build the new state from it. The tempting shortcut of updating the variables in place, one after the other, is a different (and wrong) method:

```cpp
// Wrong: I and R are updated with the NEW value of S.
S += h * (-beta * S * I);
I += h * (beta * S * I - gamma * I);   // uses S after this step's infections
R += h * (gamma * I);                  // uses I after this step's update
```

The infections removed from $S$ are no longer the infections added to $I$, so $S + I + R$ is no longer conserved, and the result depends on the order in which the lines are written. The same trap in discrete-time models is the subject of <Concept id="update-order" />.

### Integrating to an end time

A driver calls the stepper repeatedly until the end time $t_1$. If $h$ does not divide $t_1 - t_0$ exactly, the last step must be shortened so that the solution ends exactly at $t_1$. Counting the steps in advance avoids two classic floating-point problems:

```cpp
const int n = static_cast<int>(std::ceil((t1 - t0) / h - 1e-9));  // number of steps
double t = t0;
for (int i = 0; i < n; ++i) {
    const double step = std::min(h, t1 - t);     // the last step may be shorter
    x = eulerStep(f, t, x, step);
    t = (i == n - 1) ? t1 : t + step;            // land exactly on t1
}
```

The small `1e-9` stops rounding from adding a spurious step when $h$ divides the interval: in double precision `21.0 / 0.7` evaluates to 30.000000000000004, and without the correction `std::ceil` would schedule a 31st step of almost zero length. Setting `t = t1` on the last step stops accumulated rounding in `t` from leaving the run at, say, 0.9999999999999999 instead of 1.

### Output at regular intervals

The step size controls accuracy; the output interval controls how much you record. They should be independent: a run with $h = 0.01$ days over a year takes 36,500 steps, but daily output gives 365 rows. One clean design integrates from one output time to the next with the driver above, so output appears at exactly the requested times whatever $h$ is. Another records every $m$-th step, which is simpler but ties the output times to the step. Recording only some of the computed states is often called *decimation* or *thinning*.

<Callout kind="manual" title="Output times">
The course manual's driver writes a row whenever the integration time has passed the next output time, so rows appear at the first step *after* each requested time and their times depend on $h$. Integrating exactly to each output time, as here, gives rows at the requested times themselves, which makes runs with different step sizes or methods directly comparable.
</Callout>

## In biological modelling

- **Choose h from the timescales of the model.** The fastest process sets the scale: $1/r$ for growth, $1/\gamma$ for recovery, $1/k$ for elimination. Start with a step that is a small fraction of the shortest of these, then halve it until the quantities you report stop changing. With $\gamma = 0.2$ per day (an infectious period of five days), a step of 0.3 days already produces the visible error in $S_\infty$ found above.
- **Change only the model to change the model.** To simulate another system, write its right-hand side and initial state; the stepper and the driver stay as they are. This is what the separation of model and method buys.
- **Write trajectories to a file.** For real runs, send the rows to a CSV file with an `std::ofstream` rather than the console, and stop with an exception if the file cannot be opened (see <Concept id="trajectory-output" /> and <Concept id="exceptions" />). Record $h$ and the parameters in the file so that the run can be reproduced.
- **Expect Euler in unexpected places.** Many individual-based and stochastic models contain a hidden Euler step, for example when growth within a time step is computed as rate × duration. The same accuracy and stability considerations apply there.

## Common mistakes

<Callout kind="pitfall" title="Testing floating-point times for equality">
Writing output when `t == 10.0` fails if `t` was built by repeated additions: ten additions of 0.1 give 0.9999999999999999, not 1. Count steps with an integer, or integrate to each output time as in the example.
</Callout>

- **Updating components in place.** All derivatives must be computed from the old state before any component changes.
- **Looping `while (t < tEnd)` with accumulated time.** Rounding can leave `t` a hair below `tEnd` after the intended last step, so the loop takes one extra, tiny or full-sized step past the end.
- **Writing every step to the output.** Small steps produce enormous files. Decouple the output interval from the step size.
- **Reporting a single step size.** One run shows what the method produces with that $h$. Results become trustworthy only when halving $h$ no longer changes them appreciably.
- **Forgetting the shortened last step.** Without it the integration stops short of, or overshoots, the requested end time, and comparisons with exact solutions at that time are meaningless.

<details>
<summary>Answer to exercise 3</summary>

With the correct simultaneous update, $S + I + R$ stays at 1 to within rounding (about $10^{-15}$), because the three rates sum to zero and Euler's method adds them with the same $h$. With the sequential update the sum drifts to about 0.980 for $h = 0.3$ and 0.998 for $h = 0.03$: the drift shrinks in proportion to $h$, but it is billions of times larger than rounding error. Conservation laws are cheap and powerful checks. Every Runge–Kutta method, Euler's included, preserves linear conservation laws such as this one exactly apart from rounding, at any step size, so a drift points to a bug rather than to a coarse step.
</details>
