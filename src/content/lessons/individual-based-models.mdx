## What it is

Every model so far has described a population by a few numbers: a population size $N_t$, a vector of stage abundances, a pair of densities in an ODE, or counts of molecules in a Gillespie simulation. An **individual-based model** (IBM) instead keeps one record for every organism alive. Each record holds that individual's own state, such as its position, age, size, genotype or energy reserves. The model's rules say what happens to *an individual*, and population quantities are obtained afterwards by counting and averaging.

The running example of this part of the atlas is a perennial plant growing on a square plot. Its rules can be written in the words a field ecologist would use:

1. **Survival.** Each year, every plant counts the other plants within a distance $R$ of it. A plant with $n$ such neighbours survives the year with probability $s_0/(1 + c\,n)$: crowded plants die more often.
2. **Reproduction.** Each surviving plant sets a random number of seeds, on average $f$. A seed lands a short, random distance from its parent and becomes a new plant.

That is the whole model. There is no equation for the population size and no carrying capacity. Abundance, spatial pattern and their fluctuations all follow from the two rules acting on hundreds of individual plants.

## Why it matters

Representing individuals one by one costs computing time and makes models harder to analyse, so it needs a reason. Four situations provide one.

**Individuals differ in ways that matter.** Suppose survival depends on age, body condition and genotype. An aggregate model needs one state variable for every *possible kind* of individual, and the number of kinds multiplies with each trait. An IBM needs one record per individual *that actually exists*. Once there are more possible kinds than individuals, tracking individuals is the more economical description. The minimal example below makes this concrete.

**Interactions are local.** ODE and matrix models assume that every individual experiences the same average conditions, as if the population were thoroughly mixed. Plants, corals, territorial animals and hosts on a contact network interact with a few neighbours, and *which* neighbours depends on where each one is. An IBM can track this directly.

**Numbers are small.** Ten individuals cannot produce 10.4 offspring, and a population of three can be lost in one bad year. This *demographic noise* matters for extinction and establishment. Note, however, that a stochastic model of counts such as the <Concept id="gillespie">Gillespie algorithm</Concept> already captures demographic noise exactly as long as individuals of a type are interchangeable. Small numbers are a reason to be stochastic; they become a reason to be individual-based only together with heterogeneity or local interactions.

**The rules are what you know.** Field biologists often know how an individual behaves (when it disperses, whom it competes with, what it eats) far better than they know population-level rate laws. An IBM lets you write that knowledge down as rules and then ask what population behaviour it implies.

Neither rule of the plant model mentions clusters or a target population size. Yet when the model runs, seedlings landing near their parents form dense patches, and the population settles at well under half the size that an aggregate model with the same parameters predicts. Patterns like this, which arise from the rules without being written into them, are the subject of <Concept id="emergence" />.

## How it works in C++

An IBM has the same skeleton as every simulation on this site, with the state replaced by a collection of individuals:

```cpp
struct Plant {            // one individual and its own state
    double x, y;          // position on the plot
    int age;
};

std::vector<Plant> plants;              // the population owns its individuals
for (int year = 1; year <= years; ++year) {
    applySurvival(plants, rng);         // rule 1, applied to each plant
    applyReproduction(plants, rng);     // rule 2, for the survivors
    writeCensus(year, plants);          // population quantities: counts, means
}
```

Three design decisions follow, and each has its own lesson:

- **How to represent an individual and the population** (a struct or class, a `std::vector` of values, how births and deaths change the container): <Concept id="individual-representation" />.
- **How individuals interact**, for example how a plant finds its neighbours: <Concept id="interaction-rules" />.
- **In what order things happen**, which in an IBM is a genuine modelling decision, not a detail: <Concept id="scheduling" />.

### How an IBM relates to aggregate models

An IBM is not a different kind of science from the models in earlier lessons. It sits at the fine-grained end of a family of descriptions of the same population:

| Model | State | Assumes | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| ODE | continuous densities | large, well-mixed, interchangeable individuals | <Concept id="odes" /> |
| Matrix model | numbers per age or stage class | individuals within a class are interchangeable | <Concept id="matrix-models" /> |
| Gillespie simulation | integer counts per type | well mixed; exact demographic noise | <Concept id="gillespie" /> |
| IBM | one record per individual | only what the rules say | this part of the atlas |

Moving up the table means *aggregating*: counting individuals by type and assuming that the details lost in the count (who is next to whom, small differences within a class) do not matter. Often the IBM can be made to satisfy those assumptions, and then it must reproduce the aggregate model. For the plant model, if every plant experienced the *average* crowding instead of its own, the expected population would follow

$$
N_{t+1} = N_t \,\frac{s_0}{1 + c\,\bar n_t}\,(1 + f), \qquad \bar n_t = (N_t - 1)\,\pi R^2 ,
$$

where $N_t$ is the number of plants in year $t$, $\bar n_t$ the mean number of neighbours on a plot of area 1, and the factor $(1+f)$ counts each survivor and its seeds. Setting $N_{t+1} = N_t$ gives an equilibrium of about $N^* = (s_0(1+f) - 1)/(c\,\pi R^2)$. This **mean-field** limit is a precise test of an IBM implementation, developed in <Concept id="ibm-validation" />. Where the IBM departs from it, something the aggregate model ignores is doing work.

## A minimal example

How quickly do the possible kinds of individual outnumber the individuals? Take a diploid organism with $L$ biallelic loci (three genotypes per locus: AA, Aa, aa), 10 age classes and 3 levels of body condition. An aggregate model needs $3^L \times 10 \times 3$ state variables. A simulation of 5,000 individuals needs 5,000 records, whatever $L$ is.

<Example file="ibm/intro_type_count.cpp" mark="18-23" />

With five loci there are already more possible kinds than individuals, and with twelve there are about 16 million. Most of those kinds would have an abundance of zero at any time, so an aggregate model would spend nearly all its effort on empty classes. The program uses `std::uint64_t` because the counts grow quickly: in a 32-bit `int`, the product $3^L \times 30$ would overflow from $L = 17$ onwards, and signed overflow is undefined behaviour (<Concept id="numeric-limits" />).

## In biological modelling

Individual-based models have a long history in ecology and evolution. Forest gap models follow every tree in a stand as it grows, shades its neighbours and dies. Models of animal movement follow foragers across real landscapes. Epidemiological models follow hosts on contact networks. Evolutionary models follow genomes with many interacting loci. What they share is a population built from individuals whose state and surroundings differ.

That flexibility has a price, and you should plan for it:

- **Results are distributions.** Every run is one realisation of a stochastic process, so conclusions rest on <Concept id="replicates" /> and on summaries of their spread.
- **Many choices are hidden in code.** The order of updates, the treatment of edges and the timing of births all change results. Describing an IBM completely is hard enough that ecologists developed a standard format, the ODD protocol (Overview, Design concepts, Details; see the references), for publishing them.
- **Verification needs anchors.** An IBM rarely has an exact solution, so it is tested piece by piece and against limits in which an aggregate model must hold. This is the subject of <Concept id="ibm-validation" /> and <Concept id="testing-reproducibility" />.
- **Start simple.** Begin with the fewest rules that can address the question, test them, and add one process at a time. An IBM that contains everything explains nothing.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing an IBM by default.** If individuals are numerous, well mixed and interchangeable, an ODE, matrix or Gillespie model gives the same answer faster and can be analysed. Use an IBM when heterogeneity or local interaction is part of the question.
- **Confusing heterogeneity with randomness.** Individuals that differ in a fixed trait are heterogeneous; individuals that are identical but have different luck are subject to demographic noise. The two have different consequences and should be modelled, and reported, separately.
- **Rules that a biologist could not state.** If a rule exists only as a side effect of code (for example, which plant happens to be updated first), it is an assumption nobody chose. Write the rules in words first.
- **Reporting one run.** A single trajectory of a stochastic IBM is an anecdote. Run replicates with recorded seeds.
- **Comparing with no reference.** Without a mean-field or other limiting case to compare with, there is no way to tell an emergent effect from a bug.
