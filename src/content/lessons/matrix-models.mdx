## What it is

In many populations, what an individual does next year depends on what it is now. A seedling of a perennial herb cannot flower; a large rosette may flower next summer or wait another year; a plant that has just flowered may flower again or fall back to the vegetative state. Counting "plants" without distinguishing these *stages* throws away the information that determines the population's future.

A *matrix population model* keeps one number per stage. For the three-stage plant used throughout this lesson, the state in year $t$ is the vector

$$
\mathbf n(t) = \begin{pmatrix} n_S(t) \\ n_V(t) \\ n_F(t) \end{pmatrix},
$$

the numbers of seedlings, vegetative rosettes and flowering plants at the annual census. The model says how next year's vector follows from this year's:

$$
\mathbf n(t+1) = A\,\mathbf n(t), \qquad
A = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & 4.5\\
0.12 & 0.5 & 0.35\\
0 & 0.25 & 0.4
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry $a_{ij}$ in row $i$ and column $j$ is the number of stage-$i$ individuals next year per stage-$j$ individual this year. Read down a column to see what becomes of one plant of a stage; read along a row to see where next year's plants of a stage come from. Here a flowering plant produces 4.5 seedlings (row 1, column 3); 12% of seedlings become rosettes; a rosette stays vegetative with probability 0.5 and flowers with probability 0.25; a flowering plant reverts to a rosette with probability 0.35 and flowers again with probability 0.4. These are illustrative values, not estimates for a real species. Because stages can be skipped, repeated and reversed, this is a *stage-classified* (Lefkovitch) model; the special case in which every individual moves on by one age class each year is the *Leslie* model.

Written out, the matrix product is one equation per stage:

$$
n_i(t+1) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} a_{ij}\,n_j(t), \qquad i = 1, \dots, k,
$$

with $k = 3$ stages here.

## Why it matters

Matrix models are among the most widely used tools in plant and animal demography, conservation biology and evolutionary ecology. They answer two questions directly from field estimates of survival, growth and reproduction: *at what rate will the population grow or decline if conditions stay as they are?* and *what mix of stages will it then have?* The answers are the dominant eigenvalue $\lambda$ and its eigenvector, and computing them is a short, instructive algorithm.

## How it works in C++

### Building the matrix from the life history

A projection matrix is easiest to check when it is split into two parts, $A = U + F$: a matrix $U$ of survival and stage transitions, and a matrix $F$ of reproduction. Every entry of $U$ is a probability, and since a plant can be in only one stage next year, the entries of each column of $U$ must sum to at most 1. The shortfall from 1 is the probability of dying.

For the plant: rosettes stay (0.5) or flower (0.25), so 75% survive and 25% die. A typing error that puts 0.8 instead of 0.5 would make rosettes survive with probability 1.05, an impossible matrix that would still run. The program below checks every column before using a matrix:

### The growth rate and the stable structure

The parallel lines are no accident. If $A$ is non-negative and *primitive* (every stage can eventually produce every other stage, and the lengths of the possible cycles through the life-cycle graph have no common divisor greater than 1), the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees a real, positive eigenvalue $\lambda$ larger in magnitude than all others, with an eigenvector $\mathbf w$ whose entries are all positive. For large $t$,

$$
\mathbf n(t) \approx c\,\lambda^t\,\mathbf w,
$$

where $c$ is a constant set by the initial vector. $\lambda$ is the *asymptotic growth rate* (dimensionless, per year) and $\mathbf w$, scaled to sum to 1, is the *stable stage structure*. Repeated projection is therefore itself an algorithm for $\lambda$ and $\mathbf w$: the *power method*.

Two refinements make it a good algorithm:

- **Renormalise each step.** After many years, $\lambda^t$ becomes too large or too small for a `double`. Dividing the vector by its total after each multiplication keeps it at sum 1. Then the total of $A\mathbf n$ is precisely the growth over that one step, which converges to $\lambda$.
- **Stop when the structure stops changing, but not later than a cap.** Compare successive normalised vectors. When their distance falls below a tolerance $\varepsilon$, stop. Some matrices never converge, so a maximum number of iterations is needed as well, and reaching it is an error, not an answer.

```text
Input:  projection matrix A (k × k), tolerance ε, iteration cap itMax
Output: λ and the stable structure w, or an error

n ← (1/k, …, 1/k)
for it ← 1 to itMax do
    v ← A n                       (into a new vector)
    λ ← sum of the entries of v
    if λ ≤ 0 then error "the population vanished"
    v ← v / λ
    if ‖v − n‖ < ε then return λ, v
    n ← v
error "no convergence within itMax iterations"
```

### Interpreting λ and the stable structure

- **$\lambda < 1$**: the population declines in the long run. With $\lambda \approx 0.934$ this plant loses about 6.6% of its plants per year, and the projected total halves roughly every ten years ($\ln 0.5 / \ln 0.934 \approx 10.2$).
- **$\lambda = 1$**: each stage exactly replaces itself; the population neither grows nor declines.
- **$\lambda > 1$**: the population grows, by $100(\lambda - 1)$ percent per year.

<Figure id="matrix-models-lambda" />

The model has no density dependence, so these are not predictions that a real population will grow for ever or vanish. They describe what the current vital rates imply: $\lambda$ measures how well the population performs under the conditions in which the rates were estimated, and is often used to compare sites, years or management options.

The stable structure says that, at these rates, 61% of plants would be seedlings, 27% rosettes and 13% flowering. A field census with a very different mix tells you that the population is far from its stable structure, because of a recent disturbance or a good recruitment year, or that the matrix does not describe it. Far from the stable structure, the short-term dynamics can differ strongly from $\lambda$: in the projection above the population grew in years 2 and 3 although $\lambda < 1$.

## In biological modelling

Projection matrices are usually estimated from marked individuals followed from one census to the next: each transition probability is the fraction of plants in stage $j$ found in stage $i$ the next year, and fecundity is the number of new recruits per reproductive plant. Two details matter when you build $A$ from such data:

- **The census point.** With a census just before breeding, fecundity must include the survival of offspring to their first census; with a census just after breeding, it must include the survival of the parents. Both are correct if applied consistently.
- **Primitivity.** Strict biennials and other organisms with fixed life cycles give imprimitive matrices, for which $\mathbf n(t)$ oscillates for ever and the power method does not converge. Adding stasis, even rarely, makes the matrix primitive.

$\lambda$ is a function of every entry of $A$. How much it changes when one entry changes (its sensitivity or elasticity) tells you which part of the life cycle matters most, for example where management would have the largest effect. See <Concept id="sensitivity" />.

The same algorithm finds the dominant eigenpair of any linear recurrence $\mathbf x_{t+1} = M\,\mathbf x_t$ with a dominant eigenvalue, for example a discrete-time Markov chain describing transitions of individuals between habitat patches.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying in place.** `x[i] += …` while still reading `x[j]` mixes years. Always write into a new vector.
- **Swapping rows and columns.** `A[i][j]` is "to stage $i$ from stage $j$". Transposing $A$ gives the same $\lambda$ but a different eigenvector (the reproductive values, not the stable structure), so the error can go unnoticed.
- **No iteration cap, or a cap that returns silently.** A loop that waits for convergence must end, and a result that did not converge must not look like one that did.
- **A tolerance below machine precision.** With $\varepsilon = 10^{-20}$ the test can never be met in double precision; the loop runs to the cap. Values around $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-12}$ are typical.
- **Reading $\lambda$ off the first years of a projection.** Year-to-year ratios start at 0.43, 1.08 and 1.09 here before settling at 0.934.
- **Unchecked matrices.** Survival columns above 1, negative entries and typing errors produce valid-looking output. Check them before projecting.

<summary>Worked answer for exercise 1</summary>

The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2 - 0.7\lambda - 0.45 = 0$, so $\lambda = \bigl(0.7 + \sqrt{0.49 + 1.8}\bigr)/2 \approx 1.1066$: the population grows by about 11% per year.
