## What it is

A discrete-time model says what the state will be *next generation* (see <Concept id="discrete-time" />). An ordinary differential equation (ODE) says something different: how fast the state is changing *at this instant*, given what the state is now. The best-known example in ecology is logistic growth:

$$
\frac{dN}{dt} = r\,N\left(1 - \frac{N}{K}\right)
$$

Here $N(t)$ is the population size (individuals) at time $t$ (days), $dN/dt$ is its rate of change (individuals per day), $r$ is the maximum per-capita growth rate (per day) and $K$ is the carrying capacity (individuals). Read the equation as a rule: given $N$, it gives the slope of the trajectory. At $N = 50$ with $r = 0.8$ and $K = 100$ the population grows by 20 individuals per day; at $N = K$ it does not grow at all; above $K$ it shrinks.

The word *ordinary* means that every derivative is taken with respect to a single variable, almost always time. When derivatives with respect to several variables appear, for example time and position in a model of a population spreading along a river, the equation is a *partial* differential equation (PDE). This site deals with ODEs only.

### Solutions and initial values

A *solution* of the ODE is a function $N(t)$ whose derivative equals the right-hand side at every moment. The equation alone admits infinitely many solutions, one for each starting point, so a model is completed by an *initial condition* $N(0) = N_0$. An ODE together with its initial condition is an *initial value problem*, and every numerical method in this topic solves one: given where the system starts and how fast it changes, construct the trajectory.

### Classifying equations

Three distinctions tell you what to expect from an equation and which tools apply.

- **Order** is the highest derivative that appears. The logistic equation contains only $dN/dt$, so it is *first order*. An equation with an acceleration $d^2z/dt^2$ is second order.
- **Linear or nonlinear.** An equation is linear when the unknown function and its derivatives appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together. First-order elimination of a drug from plasma, $dC/dt = -kC$, is linear. The logistic equation is nonlinear because $N(1 - N/K)$ contains $N^2$; the SIR model below is nonlinear because it contains the product $SI$.
- **Autonomous or non-autonomous.** An equation is autonomous when time does not appear explicitly on the right-hand side: the rate depends on the state alone. The logistic equation is autonomous. Make the transmission rate of an infection follow the seasons, $\beta(t) = \beta_0\,[1 + a\cos(2\pi t / 365)]$, and the model becomes non-autonomous, because the same state now changes at different rates in January and in July.

### Systems of equations

Most biological models have several state variables that change together. The SIR model of an epidemic follows the proportions of a population that are susceptible ($S$), infectious ($I$) and recovered ($R$):

$$
\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta S I, \qquad
\frac{dI}{dt} = \beta S I - \gamma I, \qquad
\frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I
$$

with transmission rate $\beta$ and recovery rate $\gamma$, both per day. Each rate depends on the whole state, not only on its own variable. In general, a system of $n$ first-order equations has the form

$$
\frac{dx_i}{dt} = f_i\bigl(t, x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\bigr), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n,
$$

or, collecting the variables into a state vector $\mathbf{x}$, simply $d\mathbf{x}/dt = \mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x})$. The functions $f_i$ may depend on every variable and on time, in any way, provided they are smooth enough (no jumps or kinks, at least within an integration step). Autonomous systems are the special case in which $\mathbf{f}$ ignores $t$. All the methods in the following lessons are written for this general form.

### From higher order to first order

Numerical methods are built for first-order systems, so a higher-order equation is rewritten as one. The recipe: introduce a new variable for every derivative below the highest, and write one first-order equation per variable.

As an example, take a seed falling through still air. Its height $z$ (metres) obeys Newton's second law with gravity and air resistance that grows with the square of the speed:

$$
\frac{d^2 z}{dt^2} = -g - c\,\frac{dz}{dt}\left|\frac{dz}{dt}\right|
$$

where $g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ is the gravitational acceleration, $c$ (per metre) measures drag relative to the seed's mass, and the product of the velocity with its absolute value makes the drag always oppose the motion. The equation is second order, nonlinear and autonomous. Introduce the vertical velocity $v = dz/dt$ (metres per second, positive upwards) and the single second-order equation becomes two first-order ones:

$$
\frac{dz}{dt} = v, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = -g - c\,v\,|v|
$$

The state is now the pair $(z, v)$, and the system has exactly the general form above. The trick works for any equation that can be solved for its highest derivative. Setting $dv/dt = 0$ gives the terminal velocity, $v = -\sqrt{g/c}$, a useful check on any simulation of the model.

## Why it matters

### Closed-form solutions are the exception

Sometimes the solution can be written down as a formula. Linear equations with constant coefficients always have one: elimination gives $C(t) = C_0 e^{-kt}$. The logistic equation is one of the few nonlinear equations that can be solved too:

$$
N(t) = \frac{K N_0\, e^{rt}}{K + N_0\left(e^{rt} - 1\right)}
$$

One way to see why: the reciprocal $u = 1/N$ satisfies $du/dt = -\dot N/N^2 = -r\,(u - 1/K)$, which is linear, so $u - 1/K$ decays exponentially from $1/N_0 - 1/K$. Rearranging gives the formula. You can also check it directly by differentiating $N(t)$ and substituting into the equation.

Most models of interest have no such formula. The SIR model has none for $S(t)$, $I(t)$ and $R(t)$, and neither do models of competing species, gene-regulatory circuits or within-host dynamics. Their behaviour must be explored with numerical methods that compute an *approximate* trajectory step by step. Closed forms remain valuable even then: they are the benchmarks against which those methods are tested. The SIR model offers a partial one, the *final-size relation*, which predicts how many individuals escape infection altogether; the lesson on <Concept id="numerical-error" /> uses it to check an integrator.

### The model is not the method

Keep two things apart from the start:

1. **The biological model** is the right-hand side $\mathbf{f}(t, \mathbf{x})$ with its parameters. It encodes your assumptions: logistic density dependence, mass-action transmission, first-order elimination.
2. **The numerical method** (Euler, Runge-Kutta, an adaptive solver) is a recipe for turning that right-hand side into an approximate trajectory. It knows nothing about biology, and it introduces errors of its own.

A good program mirrors this separation. The model lives in one function that only computes rates; the method lives in another that only advances time. You can then change the method without touching the biology, and vice versa. It also tells you how to interpret a surprising result: if a pattern appears with one step size or method and disappears with another, it is a property of the method, not of the model.

## How it works in C++

Two decisions turn a model into code: how to store the state, and how to write the right-hand side.

**The state** is a list of numbers, one per variable. A `std::vector<double>` (see <Concept id="std-vector" />) is the flexible choice: its size is set at run time, so one stepper can serve models with one, three or thirty variables. A `std::array<double, 3>` (see <Concept id="array-bitset" />) fixes the size at compile time, which suits a program built around a single model.

**The right-hand side** is a function that takes the current time and state and returns the vector of rates. It reads the state and changes nothing. Parameters either travel in a `struct` passed by `const` reference or are captured by a lambda, as later lessons show. A convenient shared signature is

```cpp
using State = std::vector<double>;
State rates(double t, const State& x);   // returns dx/dt, one entry per variable
```

Even an autonomous model accepts `t`, simply ignoring it, so that autonomous and non-autonomous models fit the same numerical methods.

The output is a small slope table. The logistic rate is zero at $N = 0$ and at $N = K$, peaks at $N = K/2$ and is negative above the carrying capacity. For the SIR model, the three rates sum to zero: individuals move between compartments but none are created or lost, so $S + I + R$ stays constant. That conservation law is a property of the model, and a correct numerical method must respect it.

The seed model fits the same mould. Written with the shared signature, with $c$ passed in, its right-hand side is

```cpp
State seedRates(double /*t*/, const State& x, double c) {
    const double g = 9.81;      // gravitational acceleration (m s^-2)
    const double v = x[1];      // x[0] is the height z (m), x[1] the velocity v (m/s)
    return {v, -g - c * v * std::abs(v)};
}
```

## In biological modelling

The ODE lessons on this site return to three models, chosen because each has a known answer to test methods against:

| Model | Equations | Parameters | Benchmark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Logistic growth | $dN/dt = rN(1 - N/K)$ | $r$ = 0.8 per day, $K$ = 100 individuals | closed-form solution |
| First-order elimination | $dC/dt = -kC$ | $k$ = 0.5 per hour | $C_0 e^{-kt}$ |
| SIR epidemic | as above | $\beta$ = 0.5, $\gamma$ = 0.2 per day | $S + I + R = 1$; final-size relation |

The parameter values are illustrative, not estimates for a particular species, drug or pathogen.

When you build your own model, write its right-hand side first and check it before integrating anything: evaluate it at states where you know the answer (an equilibrium should give zero rates, an empty population should not grow), and check any conservation law, as the example program does. Many errors that look like numerical problems later are typing errors in the right-hand side.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing the update into the model.** The right-hand side returns rates; it should never change the state or advance time. Take the state by `const` reference so the compiler enforces this.
- **Forgetting units.** Rates have units of state per unit time. If $\beta$ is per day and the step is in hours, every result is wrong by a factor of 24. Write the units of each parameter next to its declaration.
- **Dropping the time argument.** A model written as `rates(x)` cannot later become seasonal without changing every numerical method. Accept `t` from the start.
- **Losing a variable when reducing the order.** A second-order equation becomes *two* first-order equations; a third-order one becomes three. The state vector must hold each new variable.
- **Treating a closed form as the rule.** That the logistic equation can be solved is a fortunate exception. Expect to integrate numerically, and keep closed forms for testing.

<summary>Answers to exercise 1</summary>

<details>
<summary>Worked answer to exercise 2</summary>

Introduce $y = dx/dt$. Then $dx/dt = y$ and $dy/dt = -0.3\,y - 4x^3$.

```cpp
State rates(double /*t*/, const State& s) {
    const double x = s[0];
    const double y = s[1];
    return {y, -0.3 * y - 4.0 * x * x * x};
}
```
